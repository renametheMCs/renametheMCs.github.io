Why rename the LMC and SMC?

Proper attribution has always been a cornerstone of the scientific enterprise. From modern-day discussions about scientific authorship, to famous historical disputes over scientific inventions (e.g., the Newton-Leibniz debate over the invention of calculus), to even earlier disputes over "new" territories found by European empires during the Age of Exploration, the question of "who gets credit for discovering X first?" has been at the heart of scientific discovery. This question is also what drives our push to rename the LMC and SMC.

Here, we discuss what the LMC and SMC are (including their scientific importance in historical and modern-day astronomy), who Magellan was and how the MCs got their name, and why we think the MCs should be renamed.

What are the LMC and SMC?

The LMC and SMC are the two most massive satellite galaxies of our Milky Way. For professional astronomers today, the LMC and SMC are crucial laboratories for investigating how galaxies work. Because the MCs are so nearby, astrophysicists can observe individual stars and nebulae within these galaxies, allowing them to probe physical processes in exquisite detail. Furthermore, recent studies of the LMC and SMC indicate that they may have had a dramatic impact on the evolution of our own galaxy, and their ongoing interactions with the Milky Way are still an open area of research.

However, these galaxies are not just of interest to astronomers. As two of the only galaxies that can be seen with the naked eye, the LMC and SMC have a special place in many stargazers' hearts, and communities around the world (or at least in the Southern Hemisphere) have observed them for millennia. Indeed, these galaxies play an important role in many cultures' folklore.

Who was Magalhães/Magellan/Magallanes? Why is his name attached to these galaxies?

Fernão de Magalhães (Ferdinand Magellan in English, or Fernando de Magallanes in Spanish) was a Portuguese explorer who was funded by King Charles I of Spain to seek a westward passage to the Spice Islands (today known as the Maluku Islands, part of Indonesia). Magalhães found a passage through the southernmost tip of South America, now known in English as the Strait of Magellan, and made it across the Pacific Ocean. However, he was killed in the Philippines and never reached the the Spice Islands. His crew did manage to return to Spain, and the first successful circumnavigation of the globe is often mistakenly attributed to Magalhães.

Notably, Magalhães is documented to have committed acts of violence against both his crewmates and against Indigenous communities he encountered on the way.
